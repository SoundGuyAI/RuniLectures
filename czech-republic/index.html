Field briefing

Czech Republic

A Central European market that rewards preparation, evidence and patience. Reserve is not rejection; modesty is not weakness. Starting hypotheses and the questions that verify them - not a rulebook.

Evidence beats enthusiasm "We will see" is not a yes Ask, check, adapt
English narration - coming soon

01Reading the reserve

Guides describe Czech business culture as formal at first, privacy-valuing and credibility-through-action.[1] For an Israeli used to instant warmth and instant opinions, this is easy to misread. The quiet room is evaluating your evidence, not rejecting your product.

Understatement is a signal, not a gap

Czech communication is documented as valuing modesty, irony and understatement; bold claims invite skepticism rather than excitement.[1] Working move: under-promise in the room, over-deliver in the follow-up.

Not one Prague

Guides note regional texture: Prague is international; Brno has its own tech and engineering identity; Moravia and Silesia are not interchangeable backdrops.[1] Working move: ask where your counterparts are based and what their region is known for.

Working hypothesis: the most international rooms, used to foreign pitches - which means your evidence bar is highest here.[1] Check by: asking what they have seen fail before; the answer tells you what to prove first.

Working hypothesis: strong engineering culture; technical depth is respected and expected.[1] Check by: bringing someone who can answer real technical questions, not just the deck.

Working hypothesis: manufacturing and regional firms may be more traditional: hierarchy, titles, documentation, and the company stamp (razítko) still carry weight.[1] Check by: asking how agreements get formalized before you assume a handshake is the finish line.

02The opening minutes

Formality first, warmth earned. Neither is a problem to fix - it is the sequence to respect.[1]

03Cards, clothes, gifts

Small details, checked in advance.

Business cards

  • Useful for clarifying role and authority; include your title[1]
  • Czech diacritics matter - spell names exactly; it is a cheap, visible precision[1]

Dress

  • First meetings: formal side, suit or smart business wear[1]
  • Understated beats flashy - visible status display reads poorly[1]
  • Tech and startups run more relaxed; banking, government and manufacturing less so - ask ahead

Gifts between companies

  • Gifting is described as not central to Czech business etiquette; lavish gifts can raise questions[1]
  • Modest branded items are acceptable; personal gifts fit later in a relationship[1]
  • Social flower custom: avoid even numbers and funeral-associated flowers[1] - the transferable lesson: check local symbolism before gifting flowers anywhere

Officials are different

Private-sector norms do not transfer to public officials and diplomats, who are often bound by institutional gift and ethics policies. Check with the teaching team how the ceremony gift is handled; your graded part is the preparation and the words.

04Anatomy of a Czech deal

A composite from the guides - a shape to expect, not a script to impose.[1]

Scheduling in advance, not Friday afternoon

Book ahead; respect holidays, August and Friday afternoons.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: "I'll be in Prague Thursday, can we grab a meeting Friday at 4?" That is a cottage-weekend boundary, not a calendar slot.

The gatekeeper meeting credibility test

First meetings often evaluate whether your proposal deserves to go higher; decision authority sits with senior people.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: treating a non-decision-maker as a wasted meeting. Here they are the exam you pass to reach the decision-maker.

The pitch evidence over energy

Charts, figures, technical detail and realistic claims; exaggeration is a documented turn-off.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: the visionary big-numbers opener. Lead with the verifiable claim, not the dream.

The answer "we will see"

Guides flag indirect negatives: "It is difficult" or "We will see" often signal hesitation or refusal, and silence is not agreement.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: hearing "we will see" as "probably yes". Ask a neutral diagnostic: "what would you need to see to move this forward?"

The close paper matters

Contracts, translations, signatures and the company stamp (razítko) carry real weight; allow review time.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: "let's start and sort the paperwork later." Written follow-ups and clean documentation are how trust compounds here.

05Your move: three judgment calls

No single right answers - each option is defensible and buys you something different. Pick one, see the tradeoffs, then read the facilitator note.

The pitch. Presenting your IoT sensor platform to a Brno engineering firm. Your strongest slide is the market-opportunity vision; your second strongest is the test data. Room feels quiet. You lead with…

Facilitator note: the Czech test is not "no enthusiasm allowed" - it is whether your claims survive detailed questions. Any of these works if your evidence is real. None works if it is not.

The follow-up. Great meeting in Prague. You propose the next meeting for Friday 15:00 this week, since you are flying home Saturday.

Facilitator note: all three can work. The underlying skill is noticing that calendars are cultural objects - when people protect their time tells you what they value.

The silence. You finish your pricing slide. The room is silent for a long five seconds. Nobody nods. Your read?

Facilitator note: dugri-trained Israelis treat silence as a bug. In Czechia it is often the feature - careful evaluation happening in real time. Your job is to make thinking out loud safe, not to make silence impossible.

06Calibrate: instincts you bring from Israel

Israeli business culture is documented as informal, fast, improvisational and direct.[2][3] Czech culture is documented as prepared, process-respecting and understated.[1] Both work. Know which room you are in.

Your default

Improvise, adapt live, figure it out in the room; flexibility is a virtue.

CHECK
Possible read in Prague

Improvisation can read as under-preparation. Guides say preparation itself is the credibility signal.[1] Bring the detail; improvise only on top of it.

Your default

Enthusiasm and big claims open doors; modesty sounds like weakness.

CHECK
Possible read in Prague

Documented modesty norms: realistic claims persuade, bragging repels.[1] Confidence here sounds like quiet precision, not volume.

Your default

Personal warmth fast: first names, family talk, instant informality.

CHECK
Possible read in Prague

Privacy and formality first; informality is invited, not taken.[1] The warmth comes - later, and often over a beer they suggest.

07The thank-you ceremony

Same 2-3 minute course ceremony, tuned for a Czech diplomat. The ceremony gift is coordinated through the course - confirm with the teaching team what it is and who presents it. Your graded work is the preparation and the words.

1

Precision over flourish. Pronounce their name and title exactly - diacritics included. In a culture that reads accuracy as respect, a well-said name is the ceremony's first impression.[1]

2

Quote one specific, substantive point from their talk - ideally a technical or economic one - and say what it changed in your thinking. Evidence of listening beats adjectives.

3

Keep the tone warm but measured. Big emotional language fits some cultures; here, sincere understatement lands better. One dry, gentle bit of humor is allowed - irony is a documented Czech register.[1]

4

Connect the gift to both cultures with facts, not flattery: Czech craft or engineering tradition meeting Israeli innovation. If you mention beer or crystal, know what you are talking about.

5

Ask before photos, keep it short, follow up in writing. A concise, well-proofed thank-you email is itself a cultural match.

Compliance note: diplomats and public officials are often covered by gift and ethics policies. Confirm the gift plan with the teaching team, and never improvise additional personal gifts for an official.
