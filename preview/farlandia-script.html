Open the briefing

1. The toast is direct. The answer is not.

Farlandia. A country that does not exist - and that is exactly the point. Farlandia blends Eastern European formality with East Asian indirectness, so you can practice the method of this course without memorizing anyone's rules. Three moves, one scenario, two contrasts - and the rule that travels everywhere: ask, check, adapt.

2. What it looks like in the tourist board deck.

Postcards from Farlandia. An imagined old town beside the Vrata river, the Opal Coast and vineyards in the Sarka hills. These are AI-generated promotional images, not photographs of real places. Notice how quickly a convincing visual identity makes an invented country feel familiar. Good-looking material is not evidence that the place, custom or claim exists.

3. The numbers you will practice on.

Farlandia at a glance. Every number and historical detail here is invented for practice: six point eight million residents, a gross domestic product of one hundred and forty-two billion dollars, and a capital called Vranov. The fictional profile adds a language, diplomatic relations, flights, trade and a national timeline. Your exercise is to ask which facts would matter for a business visit and what real sources you would need to verify them.

4. On the minute, except at the table

Two clocks, one lunch. Farlandians schedule like Central Europeans: meetings start on the minute, and the working day runs nine to five. But lunch, twelve thirty to two, is protected relationship time - no agenda allowed. Before you book, check Founders' Week in early March, when offices close, and the Day of Quiet Ledgers at year-end, when nobody holds meetings. The clock stays at UTC plus two, all year.

5. Firm grip. Slight bow. Read the card.

Firm grip. Slight bow. Read the card. Greet the most senior person first - a firm handshake with a slight bow of the head. Cards are presented and received with both hands, text facing the recipient, and they are read before they are put away. Titles and surnames, until first names are offered - usually after a first shared meal.

6. "We will study it" is not a yes

"We will study it" is not a yes. Farlandian partners rarely say no in the room; they protect the relationship and decide later. "We will study it" can be interest, or a courteous parking lot - ask open questions about obstacles, and watch whether the next meeting actually gets scheduled. The pause before an answer means your question is being taken seriously; do not fill it. And the junior colleague taking notes briefs the absent director - treat her as the channel, not the audience.

7. The meeting went well. What do you give?

The thank-you gift, as a scenario. First, check the policy - ministries cap gifts at thirty dinars. Wrap it twice, red and gold for luck; white wrapping is for mourning. The gift is opened later, and the thanks arrive by message the next day - that is custom, not indifference. And leave clocks, knives and chrysanthemums at home.

8. Giving critical feedback

Same moment, three rooms. An Israeli tells you straight: this does not work, and here is why. An American wraps the negative inside three positives. In Farlandia, praise happens in the room and corrections arrive on paper the next day, copied to no one junior. Your move: circulate drafts and invite written comments - never correct across the table.

9. Three ways to hear "no"

Three ways to hear no. Israel: "No, and here is why." The United States: "Let's table that one." Farlandia: "We will study it and let you know" - followed by a question about your family. A change of subject after a polite maybe usually means no. Give a face-saving exit, ask about obstacles, and watch the calendar.

10. How to present in Farlandia

Presenting in Farlandia: send the brief early, and bring it printed. A bilingual deck with full titles signals respect; a bound paper copy still carries authority - one per seat, plus two for the gatekeepers. Keep video under two minutes. Hospitality opens the room before content does.

11. Gifts, and who is in the room

Gifts, and who is in the room. Modest, wrapped twice, reciprocated around the New Year. Titles and seniority lead the meeting; the younger harbor cohort is informal online but defers to elders in the room. Calibrate live - ask the host how their team splits.

12. Bow slightly. Hear the maybe.

Quick reference. Do arrive on the minute, accept the first toast, take the card with both hands, and hear "we will study it" as a maybe. Do not fill a respectful silence, do not give clocks, knives or chrysanthemums, do not clink before the host's toast, and do not use first names until you are invited. Every item here is invented - practice the pattern, and verify it in the real country.

13. A practice country, on purpose

Why an invented country? Because you cannot look Farlandia up. All that works here is the method: ask, check, adapt - and verify before you act. When your target country is real, build the briefing from named, checkable sources, like the other four decks in this kit. That is the whole lesson.
