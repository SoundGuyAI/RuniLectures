Field briefing - Czech Republic

Book it weeks ahead.
Titles first.

A working visit to Prague, planned from Czech and international trade sources - not guesses.[1][2]

Ask - Check - Adapt
Move 1 - Plan the calendar

Slow to book, sharp on time

Start arranging meetings several weeks before your visit - impromptu meetings are unwelcome - and be punctual or even early.[1] Confirm a few days before, and first contact a named person by formal email.[2]

MON-THU
Meeting windows - business day about 8:00-16:30[1]
FRI PM
Avoid Friday afternoons - many Czechs leave early for country cottages ("chata")[1][2]
JUL-AUG
Summer and Christmas/Easter weeks are harder to book[1]
Move 2 - Greetings and titles

Dobry den, pane inzenyre

Open with "Dobry den", a handshake, and pan/pani plus surname and title - and wait to be invited to first names.[2][3] A serious demeanor is respect for the visitor and the deal, not coldness.[1] Dress conservatively at first; small talk: hockey, football, technology - not politics.[2][3]

Illustrative scenario
Subject: Meeting request - solar component supply
Dear Ing. Novak, I would like to request a meeting during the week of...
Dobry den, thank you for your message. We can meet on Tuesday at 9:00.
Please confirm a few days before. Na shledanou.
Move 3 - Relationship before the deal

No quick-sale push

A flurry of quick meetings and a barrage of emails is more likely to fail - too much emphasis on an immediate sale puts Czech partners off.[1]
Expect slower, procedural decisions made at senior levels.[2]
A business lunch is formal and multi-course - rushing through it is considered ill-mannered.[1]
Choose and support a local agent; demonstrate after-sales service capability. Czech buyers are very price-sensitive.[4]
Bring literature in Czech or dual Czech/English, metric units; do not assume English outside Prague.[3][4]
Contrast - Same moment, three rooms

Giving critical feedback

ISRAEL
Illustrative scenario
"This section is wrong. Fix it before Thursday."

Direct feedback culture: Israelis "skip the positives and get straight to the point"[5]; meetings are informal and interruptions read as engagement.[6]

Move: hear the content, not the volume. Answer with data.

UNITED STATES
Illustrative scenario
"Overall it was excellent - a few small tweaks to this part."

Americans tend to precede negative feedback with positive comments; the negative can be the key point.[5][7]

Move: same check as in every room - ask exactly what needs to change, and watch the follow-through.

CZECHIA
Illustrative scenario
"We will look at it once more and let you know."

One German chamber guide describes Czech communication as often more indirect - a single outside view, so treat it as a hypothesis to check, not a rule.[3]

Move: ask who decides and by when; confirm it in writing.

Contrast - Pace of the deal

Fast track vs. long game

ISRAEL
Illustrative scenario
"Can we sign this month?"

A Czech trade portal advising its own exporters describes Israeli negotiation as valuing speed: lose momentum and counterparts move on.[8]

Move: match the tempo; come ready to decide.

UNITED STATES
Illustrative scenario
"Let's get to terms this quarter."

US negotiations are described as direct, pragmatic and outcome-focused, supported by data; "yes" is generally understood as commitment.[9]

Move: bring the numbers; confirm the next step in writing.

CZECHIA
Illustrative scenario
"Let us meet again after the holidays."

Relationship before the deal: quick-sale pressure backfires; decisions run through procedure and seniority.[1][2]

Move: invest in the relationship; patience is part of the offer.
