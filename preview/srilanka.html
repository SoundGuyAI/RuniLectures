Move 1 - Plan around the moon

Twenty holidays and a monthly full moon

Sri Lanka observes about 20 public holidays a year across Buddhist, Hindu, Muslim and Christian traditions, and each full moon is observed as Poya Day, a Buddhist religious holiday.[1] The business day generally runs 8:30 to 17:00, Monday to Friday, and the clock stays at GMT+5:30 all year.[1]

BEFORE BOOKING
Check the holiday calendar - about 20 public holidays across four faiths[1]
FULL MOON
Poya Day: every full moon is a Buddhist religious holiday[1]
DAY PLAN
Business hours generally 8:30-17:00, Monday to Friday[1]
THE CLOCK
GMT+5:30 year-round - no daylight-saving surprises[1]
Move 2 - Greetings, cards and tea

Read the card. Sip the tea.

Greet the most senior person first.[2] Business cards are essential:[1] present and receive with both hands - or the right hand supported by the left - and take a moment to read the card before putting it away.[2] When tea is offered, accepting at least a small amount generally communicates appreciation.[2]

Illustrative scenario
Good morning, Mr. Perera. Thank you for the tea.
You are welcome. How was your journey?
Smooth, thank you. And how is your family?
Very well, thank you. Now - tell us about your proposal.
Move 3 - Reading the answer

"We will try" is not a yes

First meetings test reliability and respect; trust builds through repeated interaction, and a warm meeting is not final approval - find out who recommends, influences, approves and implements.[2] Tap a card.

Giving critical feedback

Illustrative scenario

Dugri - speaking straight - is read as a sign of respect and engagement, not hostility.[7] Meyer counts Israel among the cultures where managers "tell it like it is".[5]

Move: hear the content, not the volume. Answer with data.

UNITED STATES
Illustrative scenario
"Overall it was excellent - a few small tweaks to this part."

Americans often give three positives for every negative, even when the negative is the key point; feedback is direct but framed positively.[5][8]

Move: same check as in every room - ask exactly what needs to change, and watch the follow-through.

SRI LANKA
Illustrative scenario
(Raises it privately, after the meeting.)

Criticism is normally delivered privately rather than in front of colleagues, focused on the practical problem rather than the person.[2]

Move: invite private follow-up; make it safe to raise problems one-to-one.

Contrast - Saying no

Three ways to hear "no"

ISRAEL
Illustrative scenario
"No, and here is why."

Israeli counterparts arrive well prepared and are very direct; style varies by industry, from startup to public sector.[6][7]

Move: take the direct answer as a working basis, not a slight.

UNITED STATES
Illustrative scenario
"Let's table that one."

In US business culture, "yes" is generally understood as commitment, not politeness; explicit messages are the norm.[8]

Move: ask for the explicit answer and the next step in writing.

SRI LANKA
Illustrative scenario
"We will try."

Direct refusal is often avoided; "we will see" or "it may be difficult" can carry more caution than the words suggest.[2]

Move: ask open questions about obstacles; avoid ultimatums and visible frustration.

Field guide - Gifts and generations

Gifts, and who is in the room

Modest, religiously aware, and separate from the deal.[2][3]

Modest, after a signed deal or a home invitation.[2] Chocolates, company products, or something small from Israel.[2] Alcohol only if the recipient drinks; skip alcohol or pork for Muslim hosts; leather can offend Hindu hosts.[2] Both hands; opened later; likely reciprocated.[2]

Elders and titles carry weight; seniority leads the meeting.[2] Sri Lankan studies cover Gen Y leadership barriers and how young supervisors defer to older staff.[9] Cohort labels still vary by study; some "generational" gaps are age or life-stage effects.[10]
