1. The AI Workflow

The AI Workflow. This session shows the loop behind the kit you just used - the research, copy, design, code and repo were produced by AI agents steered by a human. You are the editor-in-chief. The loop has eight steps: brief, research, prototype, critique, revise, verify, publish, media.

2. From answers to agents

First, the shift: from answers to agents. Then, you asked a chatbot for text, copied it, and did the real work yourself - the AI was a smart typewriter. Now, you describe an outcome; an agent plans, executes across tools, and reports back with evidence. Your job shifts to direction, review and judgment. Quality now depends on how well you brief, check and correct.

3. Agents write the code

Agents write the code. Claude Code, Codex and Cursor help with implementation, but the task still needs a clear boundary and a review. Ask for a concrete change, inspect the result, and test it. Do not treat a confident explanation as proof that the program works. Pick the tool for the job, and keep the decision about accepting the work with the human.

4. Assistants you just text

Assistants you just text. The personal-assistant examples on this slide show another way to delegate: start with the outcome in ordinary language, then review what comes back. This kit began through a conversation rather than a design handoff. The lesson is to make the audience, deliverable and limits clear. Background work still needs a way to report progress and bring decisions back to you.

5. Video generators: you direct, it renders

Video generators: you direct, it renders. This slide groups filmmaking tools, routes for automating generated clips, storyboarding tools and code-based motion graphics. They solve different problems. For your assignment, decide whether you need a scene, a sequence or an animated explanation before choosing. Prototype one short piece, check the facts and the sound, and review it before making the rest.

6. Steps 1-4: brief to critique

The loop that built this kit, steps one to four. Brief: define the audience, the countries and the learning goal. Research: find named sources before drafting claims. Prototype: make an early version so there is something concrete to test. Critique: ask whether it stereotypes, hides uncertainty or teaches rules instead of judgment. A useful review says exactly what must change.

7. Steps 5-8: revise to media

Steps five to eight. Revise: turn broad rules into hypotheses, verification questions and scenarios with tradeoffs. Verify: check the claims and inspect the actual presentation on a phone and a desktop. Publish: make the reviewed version available with human approval. Media: check the narration and any video against the final material. The human keeps responsibility for what students receive.

8. The briefs behind this kit

The briefs behind this kit. The initial brief asked for four country briefings and an AI session, delivered as lively interactive sites rather than static slides. The fictional practice country came later. A second steering moment asked for a do-and-don't slide in every country deck during the lecture. A third asked us to compare the kit with the diplomats' own material. A fourth pushed for fewer words, stronger visuals and a QR link. The lesson: start with the experience, then improve it through specific corrections and real benchmarks.

9. Prompted from the audience

Prompted from the audience. One request during the lecture became a sourced do-and-don't slide in each of the four country briefings. The important pattern is a short request, a concrete result and a human review. You do not have to predict every change at the start. Keep presenting, inspect what comes back, and approve the change before it goes live.

10. Workflow, not gambling

The rules of the loop. Sources or it didn't happen: AI writes drafts; it also confabulates - verification is your job. Disclose the AI: this kit discloses its AI use on every page - disclosure is professionalism, not confession. Review before scale: never mass-produce what you haven't inspected. And the human owns the decision: budgets, sends and merges stay with the human.

11. Confirmed.

Confirmed. Comparing the AI briefings with the diplomats' own material found overlap on hierarchy and titles, relationships before business, indirect answers, modest gifts and punctuality. Agreement is useful evidence, but it is not a guarantee about every person you will meet. Keep the qualifiers and ask what applies to your particular visit.

12. Missing.

Missing. The people in the room supplied details a general country briefing missed: who introduces you, when to email and then call, what written company profile to bring, what happens around food, and who else should be invited. This is why the country summary is a starting point. Local knowledge turns broad advice into a workable meeting plan.

13. Flattened.

Flattened. The diplomats distinguish between generations, regions and companies of different ages. A short AI briefing can flatten those differences into a portable rule. Use that briefing as a draft, then let the human source correct it. Ask which group the advice describes, when it was observed and what exceptions matter to your meeting.

14. Colombia comes later. AI vs. reality.

AI drafts the map. People are the territory. The Colombian ambassador's presentation gives you another chance to compare a generated briefing with a person's account of her country. Listen for what matches, what is missing and what needs a qualifier. Keep the corrections, not just the parts that confirm the draft.

15. Run the same loop for your clip

Your turn. Run the same loop for your clip. First, read between the lines of the diplomat's deck and your notes. Then research the cultural claims and gift ideas. Brief the clip with its audience, country, key points, tone and length. Make a prototype, identify what is wrong, revise and verify. Watch the final render, disclose the AI use and put references in the notes.

16. Where this comes from

Where this comes from: the tool descriptions come from each tool's official page, with no pricing or version claims, because those change. This session itself was researched, written and built by AI agents steered by a human, and reviewed on real renders. That is the loop, applied to itself.
