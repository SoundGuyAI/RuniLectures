Sri Lanka. You have four working days in Colombo, and a market to enter carefully. Here is how trade and cross-cultural sources say to plan it. Three moves, one scenario, two contrasts - and one rule that travels everywhere: ask, check, adapt.

Plan around the moon. Sri Lanka's calendar includes Poya - a full-moon holiday every month - alongside Buddhist, Hindu, Muslim and Christian festivals, about twenty public holidays a year in all. Check the calendar before you fix dates, and do not schedule a celebratory delivery for a Poya day.

Read the card. Sip the tea. Guides advise greeting the most senior person first. Business cards are essential in first meetings: present and receive with both hands, or the right hand supported by the left, and take a moment to read the card before putting it away. When tea is offered, accepting at least a small amount communicates appreciation.

"We will try" is not a yes. First meetings test whether you are reliable and respectful; trust builds through repeated interaction, and a warm meeting is not final approval - find out who recommends, who influences, who approves. "We will try" can be goodwill without a guarantee, and the sideways head waggle can mean acknowledgment, not approval. Selling in? trade.gov's guide says most US firms entering Sri Lanka require a local partner - an agent or distributor is generally the most effective way to compete. Pitch the distributor as much as the end user, and do due diligence.

The thank-you gift, as a scenario. The meeting went well. First, check the recipient's gift policy - a local gifting vendor notes many employers cap what staff can accept. Keep it modest: a small item from home, offered with the right hand or both hands. When is it opened? The sources disagree - follow your host's lead. These are practice patterns to check, not rules for everyone.

Same moment, three rooms, three illustrative scenarios. In Israel, dugri - speaking straight - is read as respect. In the US, criticism usually arrives wrapped in positives. In Sri Lanka, criticism is normally delivered privately, aimed at the practical problem rather than the person. Everywhere: ask what needs to change, and make it safe to raise problems one-to-one.

Hearing no, in three more scenarios. An Israeli counterpart may say no straight out; argument is part of the conversation. An American "yes" is generally meant as a commitment - but in every room, the real test is what survives follow-up. A Sri Lankan counterpart may protect the relationship with a polite deferral; disagreement often arrives indirectly. These sample lines are teaching scenarios, not documented quotes. Your move in each room: protect the relationship, and ask exactly what happens next.

Everything you just heard comes from named business and culture sources, on screen now, with their dates and qualifiers. The illustrations are AI-generated and labeled. Sri Lanka is multi-ethnic and multi-religious - Sinhalese, Tamil, Muslim, Burgher and more - so practices vary, and before you act on any of this: ask, check, adapt.
