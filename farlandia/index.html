Move 1 - Two clocks, one lunch

On the minute, except at the table

Farlandians schedule like Central Europeans and eat like East Asians. Meetings start on the minute, but lunch from 12:30 to 14:00 is protected relationship time with no agenda allowed. The calendar includes Founders' Week in early March, when offices close, and the Day of Quiet Ledgers at year-end, when nobody holds meetings. Farlandia Keeping Time stays at UTC+2 all year.

Move 2 - The half-bow handshake

Firm grip. Slight bow. Read the card.

Greet the most senior person first, with a firm handshake and a slight bow of the head. Business cards matter: present and receive with both hands, text facing the recipient, and take a moment to read the card before putting it away. Use titles and surnames - Director, Engineer, Professor - until first names are offered, usually only after a first shared meal.

Move 3 - Reading the answer

"We will study it" is not a yes

First meetings test reliability and respect. Farlandian partners rarely say no in the room - they protect the relationship and decide later, so find out who recommends, who influences and who signs. Tap a card.

Method - Why Farlandia is invented

A practice country, on purpose

Farlandia does not exist. Every fact, name, custom and number in this briefing was invented for the course. There are no sources to cite, deliberately.

The blend: Eastern European formality - titles, punctuality, toasts, printed authority - mixed with East Asian indirectness: face-saving, both-hands cards, comfortable silence, gifts opened later. The mix is the training: patterns you half-recognize, so you cannot run on autopilot.

The point: you cannot look Farlandia up. All that works here is the method - ask, check, adapt - plus the habit of verifying before you act.

When the country is real, do what this kit does for Colombia, Czechia, Nigeria and Sri Lanka: build the briefing from named, checkable sources, and mark every claim.
