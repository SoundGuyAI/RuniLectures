Sri Lanka

An island market where hierarchy, personal trust and careful communication shape the deal. Formal on the surface, relationship-driven underneath. Starting hypotheses and the questions that verify them - not a rulebook.

01Two systems at once

Guides describe Sri Lankan business as running on two connected systems: the visible world of titles, meetings and contracts, and the less visible world of trust, seniority and carefully interpreted messages.[1] You need both.

Hierarchy is load-bearing

The person gathering information may not be the person who recommends, approves or implements.[1] Working move: map the real decision path early and respectfully: "who else will be part of this decision?"

Face is a business asset

Guides stress dignity and avoiding public criticism: a direct "no" or public contradiction can damage the relationship the deal depends on.[1] Working move: disagree privately, offer exits, and never put a counterpart on the spot in front of their senior.

Working hypothesis: more formal environments - dress, titles and protocol matter most here.[1] Check by: asking about dress and address expectations before the first meeting.

Working hypothesis: internationally fluent and more relaxed in style - but hierarchy and seniority still shape who decides.[1] Check by: don't mistake casual dress for flat structure; ask how decisions get made.

Working hypothesis: Poya days (monthly full-moon Buddhist observances) and religious holidays shape the business calendar.[1] Check by: checking the Poya calendar before booking meetings or deadlines.

03Cards, clothes, gifts

Small details, checked in advance.

Business cards

  • Present and receive with both hands, or the right hand supported by the left; read the card before putting it away[1]
  • Never write on a card or pocket it carelessly; include your qualifications and make your decision authority clear[1]
  • A Sinhala or Tamil translation side helps with local audiences[1]

Dress

  • Conservative business dress; suits for formal and senior settings, adapted to heat and humidity[1]
  • Government, banking and legal rooms run more formal than tech and creative ones[1]
  • Temples and traditional homes require particularly modest dress; carry a light layer for strong air conditioning[1]

Gifts between companies

  • Modest, appropriate, and clearly separate from commercial influence; fitting after a completed project or a home invitation[1]
  • No alcohol unless confident the recipient drinks; avoid alcohol and pork for Muslim recipients; leather may be unsuitable for some Hindu recipients[1]
  • Give and receive with both hands or the right hand; gifts may be opened later and are often reciprocated later[1]

Officials are different

Private-sector norms do not transfer to public officials and diplomats, who are often bound by institutional gift and ethics policies. Check with the teaching team how the ceremony gift is handled; your part is the preparation and the words.

04Anatomy of a Sri Lankan deal

A composite from the guides - a shape to expect, not a script to impose.[1]

The welcome tea before terms

Refreshments and conversation open the relationship; accepting hospitality is the first signal of respect.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: "coffee's fine, let's talk numbers." Here the welcome ritual is the meeting's foundation.

The mapping who decides?

Information-gatherers, recommenders and approvers are often different people; seniority shapes who speaks freely.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: pitching only to the friendly person who replies fastest. Find the senior decision path early.

The pitch professional and patient

English is widely used in professional settings; expect careful, indirect responses rather than instant reactions.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: reading a quiet, polite room as a closed deal. Silence and waggles are not approvals.[1]

The answer face-saving no

Guides describe indirectness around refusal: hesitation may arrive as delay, ambiguity or silence rather than "no".[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: forcing a yes/no on the spot. Offer a face-saving exit: "if timing is wrong this quarter, what would need to change?"

The close patience + paper

Confirm agreements in writing; visitors should be punctual while building flexibility around travel, consultation and holidays.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: chasing daily. Consistent, courteous follow-up beats pressure.

05Your move: three judgment calls

No single right answers - each option is defensible and buys you something different. Pick one, see the tradeoffs, then read the facilitator note.

The waggle. You present your pricing. Your counterpart smiles, waggles their head side to side, and says nothing. You read this as…

Facilitator note: the lesson is not "never trust gestures" - it is that cross-cultural body language needs verbal confirmation, always.

The error. In a meeting with your partner's team, their senior director states a figure you know is wrong. Their whole team is present.

Facilitator note: dugri culture says truth now. Face-conscious cultures ask: whose truth, in front of whom, at what cost to the relationship? Both care about accuracy - they differ on staging.

The silence. Three weeks after a strong Colombo meeting, your follow-ups get warm, short replies but no decision. You…

Facilitator note: silence here is often process, not rejection. Your job is to make the internal "yes" easier to reach, not to demand it louder.

07The thank-you ceremony

Same 2-3 minute course ceremony, tuned for a Sri Lankan diplomat. The ceremony gift is coordinated through the course - confirm with the teaching team what it is and who presents it. Your work is the preparation and the words.

1

Open with respect for the office. Full title - Your Excellency where applicable - and a composed, formal tone at the start. An Ayubowan with joined palms is a graceful touch if you can do it naturally.[1]

2

Cite one substantive point from their talk and connect it to your team's project. Precision shows the listening was real.

3

Keep praise dignified, not gushing. Formality first, warmth second - the room will tell you how quickly to relax.

4

Connect the gift to both cultures with something specific - tea heritage, cricket, maritime trade history meeting Israeli innovation. Check the gift against the guide's sensitivities (no alcohol unless certain, no leather assumptions).[1]

5

Present with both hands or the right hand, ask before photos, and follow up with a well-proofed written note.[1]

Compliance note: diplomats and public officials are often covered by gift and ethics policies. Confirm the gift plan with the teaching team, and never improvise additional personal gifts for an official.
