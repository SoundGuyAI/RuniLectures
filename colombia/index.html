Field briefing

Colombia

A relationship-driven, status-aware market where warmth and formality arrive together. This guide gives you starting hypotheses, the signals to read, and the questions to ask - not a rulebook.

Hypotheses, not laws Region changes the tempo Ask, check, adapt

01First: it is Colombo, not Columbia

A small detail with an outsized effect: the misspelling is common enough that Colombians notice it, and many find it careless. Check every slide, email subject and gift tag before anything leaves your laptop. It is also the cheapest credibility you will ever earn.

One country, several business tempos

Published guides consistently describe real regional variation: Bogotá more formal and status-aware, Medellín shaped by Paisa entrepreneurial identity, Cali and the Caribbean coast warmer and more expressive.[1] Treat "Colombia" as a first approximation, then ask your counterpart where they work and how things run there.

Check your picture of the country

Guides note strong national pride and a modern, urban business sector, and warn that outdated stereotypes (drug-era jokes, "is it safe?") land badly.[1] Worth knowing: current travel-risk assessments still vary by region, so check official advisories for your specific itinerary rather than relying on vibes in either direction.[3]

Working hypothesis: seat of government and corporate headquarters; guides describe dress, titles and meetings as more formal here than on the coast.[1] Check by: asking your host about dress expectations before the first visit, and mirroring the most senior person in the room.

Working hypothesis: confident, entrepreneurial, proud of the city's reinvention story; still courteous and title-aware.[1] Check by: asking what the company is proud of locally - it is usually a good conversation, not just politeness.

Working hypothesis: warmer, more expressive, looser with time; the U.S. commercial guide notes coastal offices may run different weekly schedules, including half-day Saturdays.[1][3] Check by: confirming meeting windows and response expectations explicitly.

02The opening minutes

Guides agree that Colombian business culture invests in greetings and small talk before the agenda.[1][2] That does not mean your hosts have unlimited time - it means the first minutes carry relational information. Read the room, and let the host set the pace.

03Cards, clothes, gifts

Physical details where a small check in advance beats a confident guess.

Business cards

  • A Spanish side is a commonly recommended courtesy for market entry[1][2]
  • Include your title - roles carry information about authority[1]
  • Read the card you receive before pocketing it; it is a small visible respect[1]

Dress

  • Guides describe polished, conservative-leaning business dress, especially in Bogotá[1][2]
  • Israeli business-casual may read underdressed in a first formal meeting - ask your host if unsure
  • Climate and sector both matter; tech and coast differ from banking and capital

Gifts between companies

  • Sources describe gifting as more social than meeting-room; for a home visit, fruit, chocolates or flowers are cited as safe, roses a favorite, flowers ideally sent ahead[2]
  • One widely used guide flags lilies and marigolds as funeral-associated[1] - the transferable lesson: check local flower symbolism before bringing flowers anywhere
  • Gifts may not be opened in front of you[1]

Officials are different

Everything above concerns private business contacts. Public officials and diplomats are often bound by institutional gift and ethics policies. Before the embassy visit, check with the teaching team how the ceremony gift is handled; your graded part is the preparation and the words.

04Anatomy of a first meeting

A composite from the guides - a shape to expect, not a script to impose.[1]

Arrival on time, unhurried

Be punctual yourself; guides still advise patience and flexible scheduling, and the U.S. commercial guide warns about "puentes" - long weekends that swallow business days.[1][3]

Israeli instinct to manage: treating a late start or a long greeting as disrespect for your schedule.

Greetings & coffee relational opening

Courtesy and small talk first; a tinto (small black coffee) is a normal hospitality gesture.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: opening the deck in minute one. Deal talk from minute zero is normal at home; here it can read as impatience with the person.

The agenda a starting point

Guides describe agendas as flexible and meetings as taking the time they need; senior people hold decision authority, so a great meeting with a junior contact is progress, not a yes.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: expecting a decision in the room. Ask openly who else weighs in and what happens next.

Disagreement indirect by default

Sources describe indirect handling of refusal and criticism, protecting everyone's dignity; an enthusiastic "yes" can mean "I hear you."[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: dugri - pushing for a public yes/no. Gentler tools work better here: "what would need to be true for this to work?" surfaces real concerns without cornering anyone.

Close & follow-up written, courteous

Confirm next steps diplomatically and follow up in writing; guides note decisions may need approval above the room.[1]

Israeli instinct to manage: reading warmth as a signed deal. Send the friendly written summary and let the process run.

05Your move: three judgment calls

No single right answers - each option is defensible and buys you something different. Pick one, see the tradeoffs, then read the facilitator note.

The pitch. Two minutes into your solar-panel presentation in Bogotá, the CEO asks about your family and your trip. Your deck has 40 slides; your flight home is tomorrow.

Facilitator note: the skill is not picking the "polite" option - it is noticing which conversation the senior person wants, and having a pitch flexible enough to survive any of the three. A hard stop (flight, next meeting) is a legitimate thing to say out loud; saying it warmly and early is what makes it legitimate.

The gift. After a successful week you are invited to your partner's home for dinner in Medellín. You want to bring something.

Facilitator note: the graded skill is the check-before-you-buy habit, not the object. And note the boundary: this is a private dinner with a business partner. For public officials - like the diplomats visiting the course - institutional gift rules apply and the course coordinates the gift; your job is the ceremony, not the purchase.

The non-answer. You ask the procurement director: "Can you sign by Friday?" He smiles warmly: "Everything looks very interesting, we will be in touch."

Facilitator note: indirect communication is not dishonesty; it is a different allocation of face. Your tools are open questions, written confirmations, and time - not pressure.

06Calibrate: instincts you bring from Israel

Israeli business culture is documented as direct, informal, fast and flat - first names, open argument, decisions in the room.[4][5] Those are strengths. They are also defaults worth checking at the door.

Your default

Dugri: say it straight, argue openly, interrupt with enthusiasm, disagree with the boss in front of everyone.

CHECK
Possible read in Bogotá

Public contradiction can cost someone dignity; guides recommend private disagreement and indirect phrasing.[1] Keep the honesty, move the venue.

Your default

Business casual everywhere; first names instantly; "let's skip the ceremony and talk numbers."

CHECK
Possible read in Bogotá

Formality signals respect for the person and the occasion.[1] Dress and titles are cheap; correcting a first impression is not.

Your default

Fast pace, fast decisions; a meeting that ends without a decision feels wasted.

CHECK
Possible read in Bogotá

Relationship-building meetings are progress in a relationship-driven market.[1] Measure the first trip in trust and information, not signatures.

07The thank-you ceremony

Course context: after the diplomat's talk, your team runs a 2-3 minute thank-you ceremony with a gift coordinated through the course - confirm with the teaching team what the gift is and who presents it. The assignment rewards preparation and personalization - this structure works for the Colombian embassy visit and adapts to the others.

1

Open with one sentence in Spanish ("Muchas gracias por estar con nosotros…"), correctly pronounced. Effort is the message.

2

Quote one specific thing they said back to them. It proves listening - the exact skill this course is testing.

3

Connect the gift to both cultures - for example Colombian craft or coffee heritage meeting Israeli innovation. Once you know what the course gift is, your added value is the story around it.

4

Address them by title, thank the most senior person first, and let them set the warmth level - no forced hugs.

5

Ask before the photo, take it, and send it afterwards with a short thank-you note. The follow-up is part of the assignment.

Compliance note: diplomats and public officials are often covered by gift and ethics policies. Confirm the gift plan with the teaching team, and never improvise additional personal gifts for an official.
